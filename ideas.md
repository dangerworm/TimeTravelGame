# Ideas

These are things I've thought of which aren't yet in the design document.

## Machine cards that build on each other visually

Rather than stage cards simply stacking on top of one another, design them so they visually
interlock or extend — each card reveals a new component above or below the previous one, telling the
story of the machine's development as a physical object. When all four stages are stacked the
machine looks complete and cohesive. Stage 1 might show a bare frame; Stage 4 shows a refined,
miniaturised portal.

Pure aesthetic/production design — not relevant to the prototype, but worth pursuing for a final
version to give the machine a tactile presence on the personal board.

## Future era (deferred)

A sixth era — destinations beyond the game's present (~2125 AD) — is thematically compelling but
requires a completely different design approach. Past eras are fixed; the future is probabilistic. A
theoretical physicist would be required to calculate which probable future is stable enough to
visit. Content would be entirely invented rather than drawn from historical record.

Possible framing: "possible futures" rather than "the future." High risk (probability instability),
exotic theoretical legacy points (forecasting rather than archaeology), exotic elements not yet
available in the present. Accessing the future contributes uniquely to the Many Worlds unlock.

Deferred to a later design phase or expansion. The five historical eras are the base game.

## Destination team requirements _(partially integrated)_

Some destinations should require a minimum number of travelling team members, or a specific type of
team member at the destination. A Prehistoric era card might say "requires a physicist travelling" —
you simply cannot jump there without one assigned to the staging area. This adds a planning layer
beyond just having the right machine stage.

_Hard requirements are partially integrated: the Prehistoric era already requires a theoretical
physicist. The per-destination requirement (on individual cards rather than the era as a whole) and
the soft requirement variant below are still ideas to develop during content design._

Could also work as a soft requirement: the destination is accessible without the team member, but
has an additional risk or reduced returns if you go without.

## Back story _(resolved)_

The portal is small and exponentially unstable with size — sending a marble was possible; sending a
person is not, at least not yet. Stage 1 machines send a drone. Stage 2+ machines are stable enough
for personal travel. This explains the machine stage progression and grounds the early game
narratively.

## Real historical stuff

There are types of building materials (cement) which we know were invented thousands of years ago,
but modern scientists still aren't sure how they were made. A paper on that material could be truly
revolutionary.

We should weave in real things known to science in our world to enhance the realism of the game.

The nicest design implication of all of these is that the paper reward scales with how unknown the
thing is. Roman concrete gets a decent flip reward. Silphium — an extinct plant nobody has seen in
two thousand years — gets an exceptional one. You could tier the paper rewards by how genuinely
mysterious the source material is, which gives historians a reason to chase specific destinations
rather than just optimising for raw resource value.

Examples are included below.

### Materials & Engineering

Roman concrete (opus caementicium) is the obvious one — it's been underwater for two thousand years
and is still hardening. We know roughly what's in it but the exact mixing process and the role of
seawater in forming rare minerals is still debated. A paper on this would be legitimately
career-defining.

Greek fire — the Byzantine incendiary weapon that burned on water. Recipes existed, the weapon was
used in naval warfare for centuries, and then the knowledge simply vanished. The exact formulation
is genuinely unknown. Damascus steel — the blade material with distinctive watering patterns,
legendary sharpness, and carbon nanotube structures we can now identify but couldn't intentionally
replicate until very recently. The ore source and smelting process died with the craftsmen.

### Ancient Structures

The Antikythera mechanism — a Greek analogue computer from around 100 BC calculating astronomical
positions. We have the device, we've scanned it, and we still don't fully understand who built it or
whether it was part of a wider tradition of such instruments that simply didn't survive.

Stonehenge's bluestones — we now know they came from Wales, over 200 miles away, but the transport
method remains genuinely contested. Witnessing it would settle arguments that have run for decades.

The Nazca lines — enormous geoglyphs visible only from altitude, created by a culture with no known
means of seeing them from above.

### Biology & Agriculture

Silphium — a plant from ancient Carthage that was apparently a reliable contraceptive, so valuable
it appeared on coins, and was harvested to extinction by the Romans. Nobody knows exactly what it
was. Finding a live specimen would be extraordinary.

The origins of maize — domesticated from teosinte grass in Mesoamerica in a process that required
such selective breeding over such a short time that geneticists still argue about how it happened so
fast.

### Physics & Astronomy

The construction of the Baghdad Battery — clay jars that appear to be galvanic cells, predating
Volta by over a thousand years. Whether they actually generated electricity and what for is
unresolved.

The Wow! signal — a strong narrowband radio signal detected in 1977 that matched the expected
signature of an extraterrestrial transmission and has never been detected again. Going back to
observe the source would be rather significant.

### Medicine

The Plague of Justinian's exact pathogen — we're fairly sure it was Yersinia pestis but the strain,
its origin point, and why it disappeared as suddenly as it arrived are open questions.

The recipe for theriac — a legendary ancient antidote to poison containing up to 64 ingredients,
used for centuries and then lost. Ancient physicians swore by it; we don't know if it worked or why.
