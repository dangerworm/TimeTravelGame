# Ideas

These are things I've thought of which aren't yet in the design document.

## Machine cards that build on each other visually

Rather than stage cards simply stacking on top of one another, design them so they visually
interlock or extend - each card reveals a new component above or below the previous one, telling the
story of the machine's development as a physical object. When all four stages are stacked the
machine looks complete and cohesive. Stage 0 might show a bare frame; Stage 3 shows a refined,
miniaturised portal.

Pure aesthetic/production design - not relevant to the prototype, but worth pursuing for a final
version to give the machine a tactile presence on the personal board.

## Future era (deferred)

A sixth era - destinations beyond the game's present (~2125 AD) - is thematically compelling but
requires a completely different design approach. Past eras are fixed; the future is probabilistic. A
theoretical physicist would be required to calculate which probable future is stable enough to
visit. Content would be entirely invented rather than drawn from historical record.

Possible framing: "possible futures" rather than "the future." High risk (probability instability),
exotic theoretical reputation points (forecasting rather than archaeology), exotic elements not yet
available in the present. Accessing the future contributes uniquely to the Many Worlds unlock.

Deferred to a later design phase or expansion. The five historical eras are the base game.

## Destination team requirements _(resolved)_

Hard requirements exist only at the era level (Prehistoric requires a theoretical physicist). Per-card
requirements are soft: accessible without the right team member, but going without applies a modifier
(extra die, reduced returns, or both) printed on the card. Holding a staged card is free (occupies
the staging slot). Discarding a staged card costs 1 player action; card returns mid-deck.

## Real historical stuff

There are types of building materials (cement) which we know were invented thousands of years ago,
but modern scientists still aren't sure how they were made. A paper on that material could be truly
revolutionary.

We should weave in real things known to science in our world to enhance the realism of the game.

The nicest design implication of all of these is that the paper reward scales with how unknown the
thing is. Roman concrete gets a decent flip reward. Silphium - an extinct plant nobody has seen in
two thousand years - gets an exceptional one. You could tier the paper rewards by how genuinely
mysterious the source material is, which gives historians a reason to chase specific destinations
rather than just optimising for raw resource value.

Examples are included below.

### Materials & Engineering

Roman concrete (opus caementicium) is the obvious one - it's been underwater for two thousand years
and is still hardening. We know roughly what's in it but the exact mixing process and the role of
seawater in forming rare minerals is still debated. A paper on this would be legitimately
career-defining.

Greek fire - the Byzantine incendiary weapon that burned on water. Recipes existed, the weapon was
used in naval warfare for centuries, and then the knowledge simply vanished. The exact formulation
is genuinely unknown. Damascus steel - the blade material with distinctive watering patterns,
legendary sharpness, and carbon nanotube structures we can now identify but couldn't intentionally
replicate until very recently. The ore source and smelting process died with the craftsmen.

### Ancient Structures

The Antikythera mechanism - a Greek analogue computer from around 100 BC calculating astronomical
positions. We have the device, we've scanned it, and we still don't fully understand who built it or
whether it was part of a wider tradition of such instruments that simply didn't survive.

Stonehenge's bluestones - we now know they came from Wales, over 200 miles away, but the transport
method remains genuinely contested. Witnessing it would settle arguments that have run for decades.

The Nazca lines - enormous geoglyphs visible only from altitude, created by a culture with no known
means of seeing them from above.

### Biology & Agriculture

Silphium - a plant from ancient Carthage that was apparently a reliable contraceptive, so valuable
it appeared on coins, and was harvested to extinction by the Romans. Nobody knows exactly what it
was. Finding a live specimen would be extraordinary.

The origins of maize - domesticated from teosinte grass in Mesoamerica in a process that required
such selective breeding over such a short time that geneticists still argue about how it happened so
fast.

### Physics & Astronomy

The construction of the Baghdad Battery - clay jars that appear to be galvanic cells, predating
Volta by over a thousand years. Whether they actually generated electricity and what for is
unresolved.

The Wow! signal - a strong narrowband radio signal detected in 1977 that matched the expected
signature of an extraterrestrial transmission and has never been detected again. Going back to
observe the source would be rather significant.

### Medicine

The Plague of Justinian's exact pathogen - we're fairly sure it was Yersinia pestis but the strain,
its origin point, and why it disappeared as suddenly as it arrived are open questions.

The recipe for theriac - a legendary ancient antidote to poison containing up to 64 ingredients,
used for centuries and then lost. Ancient physicians swore by it; we don't know if it worked or why.

## Engineering team as collective action _(resolved)_

The concern (large homogeneous teams making the 3-action special irrelevant) is resolved differently:
a well-assembled team frees up the player's 3 actions by covering routine phases, enabling the
special. The special replaces the jump on that turn. Jump initiation remains a player action until
a Stage 3+ machine is in play and an experienced engineer or classical physicist with a Jump star
is available — at that point the team can initiate autonomously. No collective action rule needed.

## Physics slot identity _(resolved — now Reversal slot)_

Renamed to **Reversal slot**. A temporal analysis track distinct from Precision (destination choice)
and Computing (overclock/speed). Supports revisiting any destination to extract more depth — not
just failure recovery. Upgrade tiers: **Familiarity** (replay with real-time temporal feedback loop),
**Hindsight** (consequence draw reduced/previewed on revisit), **Awareness** (targeted reversal of a
specific consequence). Integrated into game-design.md.

Related idea: the undo expedition — see below.

## Undo expedition (collaborative consequence reversal)

A multi-player event triggered by a particularly severe closed loop consequence. Players cooperate
to make a targeted jump back and undo the change — rivals setting aside competition briefly because
a timeline catastrophe affects everyone. Enabled by Physics slot Tier 3 (targeted correction).

Open questions:
- What makes a consequence "bad enough" to trigger this? A threshold on score impact? A specific
  card type? Player consensus?
- How do players pool resources — do they share a jump cost, assign team members from multiple
  boards, split the returns?
- What does a successful undo cost each participant, and what do they gain? (Restoring the timeline
  is mutually beneficial but someone has to do the work.)
- Does this have to be planned in advance (staged like a normal jump) or can it be a reactive
  multi-player turn insert?

Related to Physics slot identity. Also links to the Lending / Alliance mechanic as a natural
moment for temporary cooperation between otherwise competing players.

## Team roster: size cap and dismissal _(resolved)_

**Slot count:** 8. Strategic constraint; typical team will be 4–6. Expand if playtesting shows
it's too tight.

**Dismissal (severance):** Player may dismiss a team member during the Develop phase. Costs
severance pay (currency, amount to be calibrated). Age 40+: also grants a partial retirement bonus,
smaller than the full benefit at 60+. Dismissed researcher leaves the game entirely. No disrespect
token, no action cost.

## Butterfly effect card: end of capitalism

A consequence of a past-altering jump: capitalism was never invented and the world now runs on
meritocracy. All hiring is done according to reputation rather than currency.

Mechanical effect (draft): for some number of rounds, hiring costs no currency but requires a
higher reputation threshold (double, or +X). Currency is temporarily irrelevant for hiring; reputation
is the only qualification that matters.

Needs: duration rule, exact threshold modifier, and whether it affects all players or only some.
Good flavour for a high-impact consequence card (probably a Draw 2 destination or from deliberate
interference).

## Hidden objectives _(resolved — Research Thesis cards)_

Each player draws a secret research thesis card at setup. Worth 8–10 reputation points if achieved
(to be calibrated). Event-based objectives (The Frontier, Tarnished, Maverick, Disruptor) may be
revealed mid-game at the player's option; end-only objectives (Saturated, Emeritus, Broad Scholar,
No Shortcuts, Diligent) score at game end only. Full thesis deck: 12–16 cards. Starting set
integrated into game-design.md.

## Reward dice _(rejected)_

No push-your-luck dynamic in this game — dice count is determined by destination cards, not player
choice. Adding positive faces would also break the typed mitigation system. Destination card returns
already serve as the reward channel; dice are specifically the risk channel. Keep them negative-only.

## Named researchers / sci-fi IP

Drew wants to use recognisable sci-fi characters as researcher cards where possible - Scotty (Star
Trek), Daniel Jackson and Samantha Carter (Stargate SG-1), etc. Failing that, clearly inspired
homages with different names (Monty Pott, Danny Mackson, Samantha Sartre).

IP position: using actual character names/likenesses without licensing is a legal risk for any
published product. "Clearly inspired by" characters with different names and no copied dialogue or
visual likeness is standard practice in the board game world (countless games have done this).
Whether the originals are feasible depends on whether this goes commercial.

Suggestion for prototype stage: use the real names freely. If the game moves toward publication,
replace with homages at that point.

## Security / field operative _(resolved — field training)_

No fifth team type. Instead, any team member may take a **field training** specialisation at age 40
(weapons handling, martial arts, or surveillance). Field-trained researchers travelling at a
destination can negate bane results from Die 3, preventing the Die 2 cascade. One skill per
researcher, chosen when the age marker reaches 40. Exact per-skill capabilities to be developed
during content design.
